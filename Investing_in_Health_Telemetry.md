# Investing in Health
> Complete 18-minute presentation script | Slides 1–19


---

### Slide 1: Introduction · *0:20*

Good morning everyone.
We are Group 20, and today we are presenting the Lancet Commission's report, Investing in Health, particularly
its 2024 report, Global Health 2050.
The report makes an interesting argument: health is not just a social responsibility; it is an economic investment.
It proposes that the world can halve premature deaths by 2050. We will discuss how this can be achieved,
critically evaluate the recommendations, and present our own analysis, particularly focusing on India.

---

### Slide 2: Health is an investment, not a cost · *1:00*

Let's begin with the central argument: Health is an investment, not a cost.
Normally, we measure a country's economic development using GDP. But GDP has a limitation: it measures
goods and services produced, not the value of people living longer.
For example, two countries might have similar economic growth, but one may have significantly better
healthcare and lower mortality. GDP alone doesn't capture this difference.
To address this, the Commission introduces the concept of Full Income.
Full income combines conventional income growth with the monetary value of reduced mortality.
Let's take India's example between 2010 and 2019. Income grew by 6.4% annually, while improvements in
mortality contributed another 2.5%. This resulted in approximately 8.8% full-income growth.
So, improvements in health added roughly 40% to the economic progress captured by ordinary income growth.
The takeaway is that when we measure development using GDP alone, we miss a significant part of the
progress created by people living longer.

---

### Slide 3: A 30-year argument · *0:50*

This idea of investing in health is not new. It has developed over approximately three decades.
In 1993, the World Bank published Investing in Health. In 2013, the Commission introduced Global Health 2035,
which proposed something called Grand Convergence: reducing infectious, child and maternal deaths in poorer
countries to the levels of the best-performing middle-income countries by 2035.
In 2018, Alma-Ata at 40 focused on universal health coverage through primary healthcare.
Finally, in 2024, we got Global Health 2050, introducing the goal of 50 by 50.
The important change is that the focus expanded from mainly infectious diseases to all major causes of
premature death, including heart disease, diabetes and cancer.

---

### Slide 4: Critics reshaped the report · *0:50*

Interestingly, the 2024 report also addresses criticisms of the earlier 2013 report.
Six commentaries were published in response to the earlier report.

For example, Jim Yong Kim and Helen Clark argued that the report ignored social determinants of health, such
as income, education and living conditions.
Another criticism was that it explained what countries should do but not how to implement it. The 2024 report
responds with 19 healthcare delivery modules and the Arrow mechanism for financing medicines.
There was also criticism about the lack of a simple measure for tracking universal health coverage. The latest
report uses one common indicator: the probability of dying before 70.
However, Margaret Chan's criticism remains partly unresolved. She argued that access to HIV treatment was
also achieved by treating it as a human right, not simply as a cost-effectiveness decision.
This raises an important question: Should healthcare decisions be based only on economic calculations, or
should equity and human rights matter too?

---

### Slide 5: The goal: 50 by 50 · *1:00*

Now let's understand the most important concept: 50 by 50.
It means reducing the probability of dying before the age of 70 by 50% by 2050.
The Commission measures this using PPD, or Probability of Premature Death.
Imagine 100 babies born today, experiencing the same death rates throughout their lives as the world
experienced in 2019. Approximately 31 would die before reaching 70.
The goal is to reduce this to approximately 15 out of 100 by 2050. There is also an intermediate target of a 30%
reduction by 2035.
Why use age 70 instead of life expectancy?
PPD provides one common measure across countries, is not distorted by differences in population age
structures, and captures deaths among both children and adults.
The baseline is 2019 because COVID-19 temporarily increased premature mortality.
So, remember: 50 by 50 means halving the probability of dying before 70, not increasing life expectancy by 50
years.

---

### Slide 6: Where does the world stand? · *0:50*

Now let's compare different regions.
The North Atlantic region, including Western Europe and Canada, has a PPD of approximately 15%.
Sub-Saharan Africa stands at approximately 50%, which is more than three times higher.
India is at approximately 35% in 2023, meaning roughly one in three people faces the probability of dying before
70 at current rates.
Globally, PPD declined from 56% in 1970 to 31% in 2019. So, progress has been substantial, but uneven.
Interestingly, the United States is at approximately 22%, and its improvement stagnated during the 2010s.
The main message is that the world has improved significantly, but countries such as India and those in
Sub-Saharan Africa still have a long way to go.

---

### Slide 7: Is 50 by 50 achievable? · *1:10*

An obvious question is whether this target is realistic.

The Commission examines historical evidence and finds that 37 countries have successfully halved their PPD
within 31 years since 1970.
However, at current improvement rates, most countries are not progressing quickly enough.
For example, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Iran and Türkiye are currently on track. China would take approximately 38
years, while India would take 54 years. The world average is approximately 55 years.
To achieve the 2050 target, PPD needs to decline by approximately 2.2% annually. But the world achieved only
1.3% annually between 2010 and 2019.
The report also uses the Rule of 70 to estimate halving time.
Halving time is approximately 69.3 divided by the annual percentage decline.
For example, at a 2.2% annual decline, halving takes roughly 31 years.
Another important observation is that approximately half of recent global improvement came from people aged
50 to 69. This highlights the growing importance of adult diseases, particularly NCDs.

---

### Slide 8: The 15 major health conditions · *1:10*

So, where should countries focus their efforts?
The Commission identifies 15 major conditions that explain approximately 80% of the life-expectancy gap with
the North Atlantic.
These are divided into two groups.
The first includes eight infectious and maternal conditions: newborn conditions, pneumonia, diarrhoea, HIV,
tuberculosis, malaria, vaccine-preventable childhood diseases and maternal deaths.
The second includes seven NCD and injury categories: heart attack and ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke,
infection-linked cancers and diseases, tobacco-related lung diseases and cancers, diabetes, road injuries and
suicide.
Why is this distinction important?
Countries with high infectious disease burdens need to prioritise immunisation, maternal healthcare and
infection control.
But countries that have already controlled many infectious diseases need to focus increasingly on heart disease,
diabetes, cancer and other NCDs.
These 15 conditions accounted for 86% of global life-expectancy improvements between 2000 and 2019.
For India, they explain approximately 78% of the life-expectancy gap with the North Atlantic.
This is particularly important for our own analysis.

---

### Slide 9: Build healthcare systems module by module · *1:10*

Identifying the problems is only one part of the solution. We also need to understand how healthcare should
actually be delivered.
The Commission proposes 19 healthcare delivery modules, organised into three levels.
At the foundation, there are 12 community primary-care modules, including immunisation, pregnancy and
childbirth, child healthcare, TB, HIV, basic heart and lung care, and mental healthcare.
Above these are five first-level hospital modules, including surgery and emergency care.

At the top are two referral hospital modules for specialised services, including cancer care.
The idea is to establish a strong foundation of primary healthcare and gradually build more advanced facilities.
The Commission calls this Progressive Universalism: cover everyone, but start with the highest-value
healthcare services.
It also proposes modular cost-effectiveness. First, governments decide how much budget each module
receives. Then technical experts decide the best combination of interventions within each module.
For example, treating diabetes together with preventing heart disease can be more cost-effective than treating
them independently.

---

### Slide 10: Financing and the Arrow mechanism · *1:15*

Now comes the question: Who pays for all this?
The Commission estimates that public health spending needs to reach approximately 2.5% of GDP in
low-income countries and 4.1% in lower-middle-income countries, including India.
It also recommends allocating at least 10–15% of government budgets to health.
But financing healthcare is not just about hospitals. The cost of medicines is another major problem.
Many households pay for medicines directly, which can create serious financial difficulties.
To address this, the Commission proposes the Arrow mechanism, named after economist Kenneth Arrow.
It has four components.
First, governments create a dedicated budget for essential medicines.
Second, they purchase medicines collectively through pooled procurement.
Third, they negotiate long-term agreements with suppliers and purchase in large quantities.
Fourth, they use existing public and private supply chains to distribute medicines reliably and prevent shortages.
For example, buying medicines in large quantities allows governments to negotiate better prices than
purchasing them individually.
The report provides evidence from the AMFm malaria-drug programme between 2009 and 2012, where
medicine availability increased by 26–52 percentage points.
This is particularly relevant for India, where more than 90% of drug spending is privately financed.
Public financing could therefore significantly reduce the burden on households.

---

### Slide 11: Pandemics: A predictable risk · *0:55*

The next issue is pandemic preparedness.
COVID-19 demonstrated how severely pandemics can affect mortality and economic activity.
The Commission estimates a 23% probability of a pandemic causing at least 25 million deaths within 10 years,
increasing to 48% within 25 years.
The expected annual mortality burden is approximately 2.5 million deaths, comparable to HIV, TB and malaria
combined.
The presentation also compares excess mortality during COVID-19: approximately 4% in Japan, 12% globally,
18% in India and 25% in Mexico.

The important lesson is that countries cannot wait for vaccines before responding.
They need early isolation, quarantine, diagnostics and financial support for people who must isolate.
The report also warns that influenza could represent a greater future threat than coronaviruses.
So, pandemic preparedness must be a continuous investment, not something governments focus on only during
emergencies.

---

### Slide 12: Tobacco taxation and health taxes · *0:55*

The report identifies tobacco control as one of the most important opportunities for improving public health
outside the healthcare sector.
Suppose tobacco prices double.
Out of 10 smokers, approximately two may quit and another two may reduce their consumption.
Poorer smokers tend to be more responsive to price increases, so the health benefits can be particularly
significant among lower-income populations.
For India, the 2013 Global Health 2035 report estimated that a 50% increase in tobacco prices could prevent
approximately 4 million deaths over 50 years and generate around 2 billion dollars in annual revenue.
The report also discusses taxes on sugary drinks and removing fossil-fuel subsidies.
The main idea is that taxation can simultaneously discourage harmful behaviour, improve public health and
generate government revenue.

---

### Slide 13: International health aid · *0:55*

Not every country has enough financial resources to develop a strong healthcare system, so international
assistance is important.
Between 2020 and 2022, aid for the eight infectious and maternal conditions reached approximately 64.9 billion
dollars.
However, HIV received 39%, while tuberculosis received only 5%. NCDs received approximately 200 million
dollars annually.
The report also identifies a problem called Panic and Neglect.
When an emergency such as Ebola or COVID-19 occurs, international funding increases sharply. But when the
emergency passes, funding gradually declines.
The Commission therefore recommends directing aid towards the poorest countries and global public goods,
such as medical research and pandemic preparedness.
These benefit the entire world, not just individual countries.

---

### Slide 14: Seven conclusions · *0:40*

So, let's summarise the seven recommendations.
First, achieve 50 by 50.
Second, focus on the 15 major health conditions.
Third, build healthcare systems through 19 delivery modules.
Fourth, publicly finance essential medicines through the Arrow mechanism.

Fifth, strengthen tobacco taxation.
Sixth, continuously invest in pandemic preparedness.
And seventh, redirect international aid towards the poorest countries and global public goods.
These are the main recommendations of Global Health 2050.
But the important question is: Are these recommendations equally realistic for every country?
This brings us to our critical appraisal and original analysis.

---

### Slide 15: Critical appraisal · *1:00*

The report has several strengths.
It uses one clear metric, PPD, making comparisons easier. Its recommendations are costed and selective. It
incorporates earlier criticisms, and some financing mechanisms are supported by previous evidence.
However, we identify four weaknesses.
First, much of the historical evidence comes from countries that achieved progress through relatively
inexpensive interventions against infectious diseases. Today's NCD challenges are harder and slower to
address.
Second, the report may underestimate financial limitations. Developing countries face significant debt and
limited government budgets, so doubling healthcare expenditure may not always be realistic.
Third, the age-70 cut-off leaves conditions such as dementia and mental illness outside the main target.
And fourth, although the report establishes one common target, the effort required to achieve it differs between
countries.
We wanted to examine this last issue mathematically. This brings us to our first original contribution.

---

### Slide 16: Our contribution: The premature-death hazard budget · *1:15*

We introduce something called a premature-death hazard budget.
First, let's understand the problem.
Consider different causes of death, such as heart disease, cancer, infections and accidents.
We cannot simply add their probabilities because a person can die only once.
However, their underlying death rates, called hazards, can be added.
So, total cumulative hazard is the sum of the hazards from infections, NCDs and other causes.
The relationship between PPD and cumulative hazard is:
PPD equals one minus e raised to minus H.
This comes from survival analysis. The probability of surviving up to age 70 is e raised to minus H, and the
probability of dying is one minus the probability of surviving.
Now, the Commission wants to halve PPD.
We translate that target into a corresponding maximum cumulative hazard, which we call the hazard budget.
This allows us to divide premature-death risk by cause and examine the reduction required in each category.
Our first finding is that the same target requires unequal effort.

Our analysis shows that Japan needs its death rates to decline by approximately 2.34% annually, India by
2.63%, and Nigeria by 3.12%.
Why?
Because the relationship between PPD and cumulative hazard is nonlinear. A country starting with a higher
premature-death probability needs a larger reduction in cumulative death rates to halve that probability.
One important clarification: the Commission's 2.2% target is not wrong. It is measured using PPD. Our
contribution is translating it into death-rate reductions to demonstrate the unequal effort required across
countries.

---

### Slide 17: India's premature-death risk is now mostly NCDs · *1:00*

Now we apply our hazard-budget approach specifically to India.
We use WHO Global Health Estimates to examine cause-of-death patterns in 2000 and 2019.
In 2000, NCDs and injuries accounted for approximately 37% of India's premature-death risk, while infectious
and maternal conditions accounted for 39%.
By 2019, the situation had changed significantly.
NCDs and injuries accounted for 54%, while infectious and maternal conditions had declined to just 17%.
Why did this happen?
India made substantial progress in controlling infectious diseases, with their associated risk declining by
approximately 6% annually.
However, NCD risk has remained flat to increasing. It increased by approximately 0.1% annually since 2000 and
1% annually since 2015.
This gives us a very important finding.
Even if every infectious and maternal death before 70 disappeared tomorrow, India would achieve only
28% of the way towards its 2050 target.
Therefore, controlling infectious diseases alone is no longer sufficient. India must increasingly focus on
preventing and treating NCDs.

---

### Slide 18: What must India do to achieve 50 by 50? · *1:00*

Now that we have established that NCDs are India's biggest challenge, the next question is: How quickly must
India reduce NCD mortality?
Our analysis estimates that India's NCD death rates need to decline by approximately 3.1% every year until
2050.
Let's compare this with historical performance.
Between 2000 and 2019, the world achieved an annual reduction of 1.5%, the North Atlantic 2.5%, China 2.6%,
and Central and Eastern Europe 2.7%.
But India's NCD death rates actually increased.
So, India needs to achieve a reduction faster than any of these regions historically managed.
Our analysis also examines what happens if existing trends continue.
If India's 2015–2019 trends continue, its PPD in 2050 would remain around 37%, meaning practically no
improvement.

Even using the longer-term 2000–2019 trends, PPD would reach only 30%.
But the target is approximately 18%.
This demonstrates the substantial gap between India's current trajectory and the required target.

---

### Slide 19: Conclusion · *0:45*

So, to conclude, the overall investment case presented by the Lancet Commission is strong.
However, our analysis shows that for India, achieving 50 by 50 is primarily an NCD problem.
We identify three particularly important interventions.
First, strengthening cardiovascular healthcare, because heart disease and ischaemic stroke account for
approximately 21% of India's premature-death risk.
Second, increasing tobacco taxation, because tobacco-linked diseases account for approximately 12% of the
risk.
And third, applying the Arrow mechanism to essential NCD medicines, including blood-pressure drugs, statins
and diabetes medicines, to improve affordability and access.
So, our final message is that health is an investment, and halving premature deaths by 2050 is achievable.
But for India, the next major challenge is controlling heart disease, stroke, diabetes and tobacco-related
diseases.
Thank you. We are happy to take your questions.
