## PART A: Why invest in health (slides 1–4, 0:00–3:00)

### Slide 1: Cover · *0:20*
*[Point at the chart.]*
"Good morning, we're Group 20. Our topic is the Lancet Commission on Investing in Health. In 1960, **62%** of the world died before 70. Today it's **30%**. The Commission says **15% by 2050** is possible. We'll cover the case for investing, the 2024 report part by part, our critique, and our own analysis for India."

---

### Slide 2: Health is an investment, not a cost · *1:00*
"The Commission's core claim is that health is an **investment, not a cost**. GDP doesn't capture the value of living longer, so the Commission uses a measure called **full income**: income growth plus the money value of falling death rates."

"Take India in 2010–19. Income grew **6.4%** a year. Falling mortality was worth another **2.5%**, so full income grew **8.8%**. That's **about 40% on top** of income growth, and none of it shows up in GDP."

"The 2013 report found the same pattern across low- and middle-income countries. Falling mortality explained about **11%** of growth as GDP measures it, but **24%** of full-income growth in 2000–11."

**"If you only count GDP, you miss a quarter of real progress."**

> If asked how life is valued: the value of a statistical life is set at **160 × income per person** for deaths under 75, following the Harvard Benefit–Cost Reference Case (GH2050 Table 2).

---

### Slide 3: A 30-year argument by the same two economists · *0:50*
"This argument is thirty years old. In **1993**, the World Bank published *Investing in Health*, still its **only** World Development Report on health. Larry Summers was the Bank's chief economist and Dean Jamison a lead author."

"The Commission was launched for that report's 20th anniversary. In **2013**, *Global Health 2035* called for a 'grand convergence': cutting infectious, child and maternal deaths in poor countries to the level of the best middle-income countries by 2035. In **2018**, *Alma-Ata at 40* said to deliver 'essential UHC' through primary care. And in **2024**, *Global Health 2050* set the goal of **'50 by 50'**."

**"What stayed the same: Summers and Jamison, writing for finance ministers. What changed: 25 authors became 50, and the focus widened from infections to all premature death."**

> If asked: the 2018 paper's lead authors were Watkins and Yamey, from the same Commission. Kenneth Arrow sat on the 2013 Commission, which is where the "Arrow mechanism" name comes from.

---

### Slide 4: Critics in 2013 reshaped the 2024 report · *0:50*
"When the 2013 report came out, the Lancet published six Comments on it. All six supported the investment case, but the criticisms were pointed, and you can see them answered in 2024."

"Jim Yong Kim of the World Bank and Helen Clark of UNDP said it **ignored social determinants**, so 2024 adds a **whole part on health taxes**. Kim said it explained **what to do but not how**, so 2024 adds **19 delivery modules** and the **Arrow mechanism** for financing drugs. Lancet editors Horton and Lo said **universal coverage had no simple indicator**, so 2024 uses **one number**, the probability of dying before 70."

"One point is **still open**. WHO's Margaret Chan noted that HIV drugs were scaled up by putting **rights ahead of cost-benefit**. The Commission still chooses priorities."

> If asked: social determinants are only partly answered. Income, education and cash transfers are still left out. Clark's own example was cash transfers in Malawi cutting HIV among adolescent girls by 64% within 18 months.

## PART B: Global Health 2050, part by part (slides 5–14, 3:00–13:00)

### Slide 5: "50 by 50": halve the chance of dying before 70 · *1:00*
"Imagine **100 babies** born with the death rates of 2019. **31** of them would die before 70. The goal is to get that to **15** by 2050. That's '50 by 50': **halve the probability of premature death, or PPD**, from its 2019 level. The milestone on the way is **30% lower by 2035**."

"The baseline is 2019 because COVID pushed global PPD up by more than **4 points**, and the Commission treats that as temporary."

"Why PPD rather than life expectancy? It's **one number**, it isn't distorted by a country's age structure, it **counts every death before 70**, and it shows progress even where death rates are already low. From 2000 to 2019, the North Atlantic's life expectancy rose only 5%, but its PPD fell **27%**."

> If asked why 70: global life expectancy is "just over 70", and most deaths before 70 are avoidable. The report quotes Richard Doll: *"In old age death is inevitable, but death before old age is not."*

---

### Slide 6: A 3× gap between regions · *0:50*
"Here's where the world starts, using 2023 figures. The **North Atlantic**, meaning western Europe and Canada, is at **15%**, which is already the 2050 target. **Sub-Saharan Africa** is at **50%**. That's a **3× gap**."

"**India is at 35%. About one in three Indians dies before 70.**"

"Globally PPD fell from **56% in 1970 to 31% in 2019**. That's big progress, but uneven. The USA is at 22% and stopped improving in the 2010s. India's 2019 PPD of 37% is already **below** the USA's in 1970, which was 38%."

---

### Slide 7: Feasible, but most countries must speed up · *1:10*
"Is this realistic? The chart shows **how many years each country would take to halve PPD at its 2010–19 pace**. To make it by 2050 you need **31 years or fewer**."

"Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Iran and Türkiye are on track. China, at 38 years, is close. **India would take 54 years**, the **world 55**, and the USA and Nigeria more than 75."

"The world needs a **2.2%** decline a year and managed **1.3%**. But it has been done before: **37 countries** have halved PPD within 31 years since 1970, from high and low starting points. Today, **7 of the 30 most populous countries** are on track."

"One more fact sets up our analysis. About **half** of the recent global improvement came from people aged **50 to 69**, which means NCDs."

> If asked: halving time = **69.3 ÷ the annual % decline**, the rule of 70. The 2.2% is ln 2 ÷ 31.

---

### Slide 8: 15 conditions explain ~80% of the life-expectancy gap · *1:10*
"Where should the effort go? The report picks **15 conditions** that explain about **80%** of the life-expectancy gap with the North Atlantic."

"**Eight are infectious and maternal**: newborn conditions, pneumonia, diarrhoea, HIV, TB, malaria, vaccine-preventable childhood diseases and maternal deaths. These come first where PPD is high."

"**Seven are NCDs and injuries**: heart attack and ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, infection-linked cancers, tobacco-linked lung disease and cancers, diabetes, road injuries and suicide. These matter in almost every country."

"These 15 delivered **86%** of the world's life-expectancy gain in 2000–19. For India they explain **78%** of the gap: 29 points from infections and **49 points from NCDs**."

---

### Slide 9: Build health systems module by module · *1:10*
"This is how the report answers 'how do you deliver it?' It proposes **19 modules**, drawn here as a house."

"The foundation is **12 community primary-care modules**: immunisation, child illness, pregnancy, TB, HIV, basic heart and lung care, mental health and others. Above that are **5 first-level hospital modules**, such as surgery and emergency care, and at the top **2 referral modules** for cancer care."

"Budgeting has two steps. **First, a political decision on how much each module gets. Second, a technical choice of the best mix of interventions within each module.** That captures synergies. For example, diabetes care becomes cost-effective when it's bundled with heart-disease prevention."

**"The principle is progressive universalism: cover everyone, starting with the highest-value care."**

> If asked: costs range from 0.2 basis points of GDP per +10% coverage for immunisation (cheapest) to 13.0 for enhanced cancer care (most expensive). Basic cardiovascular care is 7.1. The report calls the modules "only an example". There was a pilot in Xishui County, China, and Thailand reached a near-US PPD at a fraction of the cost.

---

### Slide 10: Pay for it: double public spending, aim it at drugs · *1:15*
"Who pays? **Governments must roughly double public spending** on these conditions. Low-income countries need to reach **2.5% of GDP**, an extra 1.1 points. Lower-middle-income countries need **4.1%**, an extra 2. Health should get **at least 10 to 15%** of government budgets."

"Why focus on drugs? Out-of-pocket drug costs push families into catastrophic spending. So the report proposes the **Arrow mechanism**, named after the economist Kenneth Arrow. It has four parts: a **dedicated budget line** for priority drugs, **pooled procurement** with long-term deals, **volumes large enough** to avoid stock-outs, and **existing public and private supply chains**."

"It has been tested. In the **AMFm** malaria-drug scheme, 2009–12, availability rose by **26 to 52 percentage points**."

**"For India this matters: over 90% of drug spending is private, mostly out of pocket."**

> If asked: the Global Fund board **ended AMFm anyway**, and prices rose once the subsidies stopped. The money can come from tax: the IMF says low- and middle-income countries could raise tax-to-GDP by up to 9 points, with 15% as a minimum. Ministries in low-income countries also leave about USD 4 per person unspent each year.

### Slide 11: Pandemics: a predictable risk the world underfunds · *0:55* *(dark slide)*
"The chance of a pandemic killing **25 million or more**, COVID-scale, is **23% within 10 years** and **48% within 25 years**, close to a coin flip."

"Spread over time, that's **2.5 million expected deaths a year**, about the same as **HIV, TB and malaria combined**. Influenza is the bigger threat, ahead of coronaviruses."

"COVID showed how much responses differ. Excess deaths were **4%** above normal in Japan, 5% in China, 12% worldwide, **18% in India** and 25% in Mexico."

**"The lesson: early isolation, quarantine and financial support for people isolating save lives before vaccines arrive."**

> If asked: the P-score is excess deaths as a % of expected deaths. India's welfare loss was 47% of GNI. The estimated need is USD 31.1 bn a year, including at least USD 10.5 bn more in international finance. Spending is skewed towards vaccines, while diagnostics and public-health measures are neglected.

---

### Slide 12: "Tobacco is the new tobacco": tax it · *0:55*
"The report calls tobacco control **by far the most important policy outside the health sector**. 'Tobacco is the new tobacco' means nothing has replaced it as the biggest single opportunity."

"**Double the price and, out of 10 smokers, about 2 quit and 2 cut down.** Poorer smokers respond most, so the health benefits are progressive."

"But only **40 countries**, home to about **10%** of the world's people, tax tobacco at the recommended **75% of the price**. For **India**, the 2013 report estimated that a 50% price rise would prevent about **4 million deaths** over 50 years and raise about **USD 2 billion a year**."

"The same part covers **USD 468 billion** of fossil-fuel subsidies and sugary-drink taxes, which 117 countries already have."

> If asked about smuggling: large-scale smuggling happens mainly when the **industry organises it** (US to Canada in the 1990s). Track-and-trace systems with tax stamps work.

---

### Slide 13: Aid: fund global goods, target the poorest · *0:55*
"Aid for the 8 infectious conditions came to **USD 64.9 billion** in 2020–22. **HIV got 39%. TB got only 5%**, even though TB is a top killer, and about half of all TB deaths are in India, Nigeria and Indonesia."

"About **half went to middle-income countries** that could pay for themselves. NCDs got only about **USD 0.2 billion a year**."

"Aid for global functions such as R&D and pandemic preparedness follows **'panic and neglect'**: it jumps after Ebola and COVID, then falls again."

**"The recommendation: aid for the poorest countries, and for global public goods."**

---

### Slide 14: Seven conclusions · *0:40*
"So the seven conclusions. **One**, 50 by 50 is achievable for countries that choose to invest. **Two**, focus on 15 conditions. **Three**, build health systems in modules. **Four**, publicly fund key drugs through the Arrow mechanism. **Five**, tax tobacco. **Six**, prepare for pandemics with public-health basics. **Seven**, redirect aid. The first six are for governments, the seventh is for donors."

*[Read the quote.]* **"'The case is better than ever for the value of investing in health.'"**

### Slide 16: A premature-death "hazard budget" · *1:15*
*[Say this before you point at the formula.]*
"**Probabilities don't add up across causes, because you can only die once. Death rates do.** Demographers call them hazards."

"PPD equals one minus e to the minus H, where **H is the sum of death rates from infections, NCDs and everything else**. Halving PPD sets a target **H-star**, which we call a **hazard budget**. We can divide it by cause and test each cause against its own trend."

"**Finding 1: same target, unequal effort.** In death-rate terms, Japan needs a **2.34%** fall a year, India **2.63%** and Nigeria **3.12%**. **The higher you start, the bigger the cut you need.**"

*[Always say this fairness line.]* "To be clear, the Commission's 2.2% is **internally consistent**. It's measured on PPD. Our point is what that target means once you translate it into death rates, and **the poorest countries face the steepest climb**."

> If asked for the arithmetic, take Nigeria: H₀ = −ln(0.37) = 0.994 and H* = −ln(0.685) = 0.378. That's a 61.9% cut, or 3.12% a year. For Japan it's a 51.6% cut.

"In **2000**, NCDs were **37%** of India's risk of dying before 70 and infections **39%**. By **2019**, NCDs were **54%** and infections just **17%**."

"Infections are falling about **6% a year**, and that drove India's past progress. **NCD risk is flat to rising**: up 0.1% a year since 2000 and **1.0% a year since 2015**."

**"Even if every infectious and maternal death disappeared tomorrow, India would get only 28% of the way to its target."**

> If asked: this matches the report's own Table 5, where 85% of India's life-expectancy gain in 2000–19 came from infections and almost none from NCDs. Our 2019 PPD is 36.0%, against the UN's 37%.

"So how fast must NCDs fall? **About 3.1% a year**, every year to 2050."

"For comparison, from 2000 to 2019 the world managed 1.5%, the North Atlantic 2.5%, China 2.6% and Central and Eastern Europe 2.7%. **India's NCD death rates went up.**"

"If India's 2015–19 trends continue, its PPD in 2050 is **37%**, no progress at all. On 2000–19 trends it's **30%**. The target is **18%**."

**"That's faster than any region managed, and India would be starting from a rising trend."**

> If asked: 3.1% assumes the other causes keep their 2000–19 trends. Under other assumptions the range is 2.7–4.6%. The regional comparison uses ages 50–69 from GH2050 Table 7, so it's indicative (flagged on the slide). The 2035 milestone already needs 2.7% a year, and India managed 0.8%.

**"The investment case holds. But for India, 50 by 50 is an NCD problem."**

"The report already has the tools. ## Q&A bank, by slide

Each answer takes under 30 seconds. ⭐ marks the questions most likely to be asked, and **slides 16–18 deserve two read-throughs.** Name the source when you can ("Table 7 of the report…"), since it sounds confident and it's true.

### Slide 2: Health is an investment
- ⭐ **How do you put a money value on longer life?** With the value of a statistical life: what people are willing to pay to lower their risk of dying. GH2050 sets it at 160 × income per person for deaths under 75 (Harvard reference case).
- **Isn't it unethical to put a price on life?** It's a way to compare with other spending, not a price on any individual. It actually *raises* health's priority, because GDP values a longer life at zero.
- **Isn't full income double counting?** No. GDP measures output, and the health part measures the value of lower risk of death. They're separate, and full income adds them.
- **Does health cause growth, or the reverse?** Both. But full income doesn't depend on which way it runs, because it counts longer life as welfare in itself.

### Slide 3: A 30-year argument
- ⭐ **Grand convergence vs 50 by 50?** 2013 was about poor countries catching up on infectious, child and maternal deaths by 2035. 2024 covers every country (including rich ones like the USA) and every death before 70, including NCDs.
- **Why does WDR 1993 matter?** It's the only World Development Report on health, and it brought cost-effectiveness analysis and tobacco taxes to finance ministers.
- ⭐ **Who funded it? Any bias?** The **Gates Foundation and Norad**, plus **JICA's Ogata Sadako Research Institute and GIZ**. Funders sat on the advisory committee. All of this is disclosed, and we raise the agenda-setting point in our appraisal.

### Slide 4: Critics reshaped the report
- **Did GH2050 really answer social determinants?** Only partly. It added health taxes, but it still leaves out income, education and cash transfers (p1596).
- **Why is Chan's point still open?** GH2050 is even more selective than 2013. Modular cost-effectiveness could squeeze out costly care like dialysis or new cancer drugs.
- **Were the 2013 commentators independent?** Partly. Horton was also a 2013 Commissioner, and the 2024 Comment came from the Commission's own Advisory Committee.

### Slide 5: 50 by 50
- ⭐ **Why age 70?** Global life expectancy is just over 70, and most deaths before then are avoidable (Doll's quote). The cost is that dementia and late-life illness don't count towards the target.
- **Why 2019, not today?** COVID raised PPD by more than 4 points, and the Commission treats that as temporary. Including COVID would make it harder: India's PPD was about 46% in 2021 (our calculation).
- **What about morbidity?** Mortality and morbidity track closely (Figure 4). Conditions that cause suffering without early death, like mental illness and dementia, get their own modules.

### Slide 6: Where the world starts
- **Why is the benchmark the North Atlantic, not the USA?** The USA is at 22% and got worse in the 2010s. The North Atlantic (15%) is the best-performing region.
- ⭐ **Slide 6 says India is at 35% but slide 7 says 37%. Which is it?** 35% is 2023, and 37% is 2019, the baseline year. Both come from the UN's World Population Prospects.

### Slide 7: Feasible, but must speed up
- ⭐ **Trap: "7 of 30 halved PPD: Bangladesh, China, Iran, Italy, Japan, Korea, Viet Nam. Why does your slide list Ethiopia and Türkiye?"** There are **two different sevens.** Historical: those seven halved PPD at some point since 1970. On track today: South Korea, Bangladesh, Russia, South Africa, Iran, Türkiye and Ethiopia are falling fast enough *now* (Table 1).
- **How is halving time calculated?** 69.3 ÷ the annual % decline. A 31-year halving needs ln 2 ÷ 31 = 2.24%, which rounds to 2.2%.
- **Isn't the list of 37 cherry-picked?** It covers every country that did it since 1970. Our critique is that most did it through cheap wins against infections (slide 15).

### Slide 8: 15 conditions
- **How were the 15 chosen?** They explain most of the life-expectancy gap and of past gains, and cost-effective interventions exist for each one.
- **Which cancers count?** Infection-linked cancers (cervical, stomach, liver) and tobacco-linked ones (lung, mouth, larynx). Breast and colorectal cancer are in the cancer modules but not among the 15.
- **Why road injury and suicide?** They're big causes of death at working ages, and there are cheap policies against them, such as road design and restricting the most lethal means of suicide.

### Slide 9: Modules
- **Modular vs standard cost-effectiveness analysis?** A standard ranking of every intervention ignores synergies and the political trade-offs between outcomes. The modular approach makes the across-module choice political and the within-module choice technical.
- **Is there evidence it works?** It's new. There was a pilot in Xishui County, China, and the report calls the modules "only an example".

### Slide 10: Pay for it / Arrow mechanism
- ⭐ **Why "Arrow"?** It's named after Kenneth Arrow, the Nobel economist and 2013 Commissioner, who designed the idea for malaria drugs.
- **What happened to AMFm?** It worked in most pilot countries, but the Global Fund board ended it. After the subsidies stopped, prices rose. That's a warning about political sustainability.
- **The bar says "implied". Why?** The report gives the target and the extra amount needed. "Already spent" is simply the difference between them.
- **Is ">90% out of pocket" exact?** Table 11 says over 90% of India's drug spending is *privately* financed, and most of that is out of pocket. If challenged, say "private".

### Slide 11: Pandemics
- **What's a P-score?** Excess deaths as a % of expected deaths.
- **Are India's COVID numbers reliable?** They're modelled excess-death estimates (from *The Economist*), with wide uncertainty. Even so, the ranking is robust.
- **Why is influenza the bigger threat?** It accounts for about twice the expected losses of coronaviruses, and it kills younger people, so measures like school closures would make more sense.

### Slide 12: Tobacco tax
- ⭐ **Isn't a tobacco tax regressive?** Poor smokers pay more as a share of income, but they also quit most, so the *health* and financial-protection benefits are progressive (Panel 8).
- **What about smuggling?** Large-scale smuggling mostly happens when the industry organises it. Track-and-trace systems with tax stamps work.
- **Where does India's 4 million come from?** GH2035 (2013): a 50% price rise would avert about 4 million deaths over 50 years and raise about USD 2 billion a year.

### Slide 13: Aid
- **What is DAH+?** Development assistance for health plus donor funding for neglected-disease R&D. It hit a record USD 47.6 billion in 2022.
- **Why move aid to global functions?** As countries grow richer they can fund their own care, but nobody pays for global public goods like R&D, pandemic preparedness or antimicrobial resistance unless donors do.

### Slide 14: Seven conclusions
- ⭐ **Which conclusions matter most for India?** Tobacco tax, the Arrow mechanism for NCD drugs and the cardiovascular module. That's our slide 19.
- **Is "countries that choose to" fair?** Partly. Choices matter, since countries with similar incomes get very different outcomes, but debt and fiscal limits are real (slide 15).

### Slide 15: Critical appraisal
- ⭐ **Is the "backward-looking evidence" critique fair? Italy, Japan and Norway halved PPD through NCDs.** Yes, we accept that, but they were rich countries with large health budgets. The report's own Table 7 shows NCD death rates falling only 1.5% a year globally.
- **What's the single biggest strength?** One clear, costed target with a concrete delivery plan.
- **Is there any conflict of interest?** All interests are *disclosed*. One worth noting: Panel 9 names a specific track-and-trace product, SICPATRACE, and one Commissioner declares consulting fees from its maker, SICPA. The tobacco-tax evidence itself rests on many independent studies.
- **Is 50 by 50 equitable?** It's a national average with no target for inequality. India has an 8-year gap in life expectancy between its richest and poorest fifths (Panel 2), and that gap could widen even while the national figure halves.

### Slide 16: Hazard budget
- ⭐ **Why do hazards add when probabilities don't?** At every age the total death rate is the sum of the death rates from each cause. Integrate over ages and you get H = Σ H_c. Probabilities can't be added because you can only die once.
- ⭐ **Where does 1 − e^(−H) come from?** Survival to 70 is S = exp(−∫μ), the standard survival formula, so PPD = 1 − S.
- ⭐ **So is the Commission's 2.2% wrong?** No. It's internally consistent, because it's measured on PPD. Our point is that when you translate it into death-rate cuts, the effort is unequal.
- **Why does a higher PPD need a bigger cut?** The relationship curves. When PPD is high, a given cut in H lowers PPD by less, so halving PPD needs more than halving H.
- **Is the idea new?** Adding hazards is standard demography (competing risks, cause-deleted life tables). What's new is using it as a budget for 50 by 50 and testing each cause against its own trend, which GH2050 doesn't do.

### Slide 17: India is mostly NCDs
- ⭐ **What data, and how reliable is it?** WHO Global Health Estimates 2021 for 2000, 2015 and 2019, which GH2050 also uses. India's death registration is incomplete, so WHO models the gaps using sample surveys. Our 36.0% against the UN's 37% is a good cross-check.
- **Why 36% and not 37%?** WHO uses coarse age bands, and its population figures differ slightly from the UN's.
- **What's in "other"?** Mainly other cancers (breast, colorectal), alcohol-related cirrhosis and digestive disease, falls, drowning, burns and congenital conditions.
- ⭐ **How do you get "only 28% of the way"?** Removing every infectious and maternal death lowers PPD from 36.0% to 30.9%. That's 5.1 of the 18 points needed, which is 28%. It's also a best case, because some of those people would die of other causes.

### Slide 18: What India must do
- ⭐ **You compare India at all ages under 70 with regions at 50–69. Is that fair?** It's indicative, and flagged on the slide. Table 7 only reports ages 50–69, which is where most NCD deaths before 70 happen. The report's own 50–69 figure for India is +0.3% a year, also rising.
- **How sensitive is the 3.1%?** It ranges from 2.7% (all infections eliminated) to 4.6% (other causes flat). Every scenario is above the best regional record.
- **Isn't 2015–19 too short?** Yes, so we also show 2000–19. Even on that longer trend, India only reaches 30% by 2050.
- ⭐ **So is 50 by 50 impossible for India?** Not impossible, but **impossible on current trends.** India would need an NCD decline faster than any region has achieved.

### Slide 19: Conclusion
- ⭐ **Why these three levers?** They target India's biggest and fastest-rising hazards (CVD at 21%, tobacco at 12%), and the report has the strongest evidence for them.
- **What about India's existing NCD programmes?** We didn't evaluate them. What our data shows is that NCD risk was still rising through 2019.
- **Why not tax sugar or junk food?** GH2050 says evidence on reversing obesity is weak and cites a study finding no excess mortality from high BMI in India or Bangladesh. CVD drugs, diabetes care and tobacco tax have stronger evidence.
- **If you could do only one thing, what would it be?** A tobacco tax. It saves lives and raises revenue from day one.
